Why I built this

Every semester, the same problem.

I am the president of TCU Rugby. Every semester, getting a purchase request approved means drafting an email, attaching whatever documents I think are needed, and waiting — sometimes for days — with no way to check whether anyone has seen it. Emails get lost. Replies do not come. What should be a simple process turns into five back-and-forth emails over two weeks.

I have heard the same complaints from officers of other competitive sports clubs at TCU. The process is confusing, requirements are not centralized, and there is no way to check the status of anything without sending another email. Game roster approvals, travel requests, budget check-ins — all of it runs through individual inboxes.

It is not just frustrating for students. The Rec Center staff deal with their own version of the same problem: clubs submit incomplete information, staff have to ask repeatedly for the same documents, and keeping clubs updated on their budget balances requires manually maintaining spreadsheets and emailing them around.

FrogFlow is my attempt to solve that — to give this information a place to live, structured around how club sports actually work. Built specifically for TCU.

Under the hood

The data behind the prototype.

FrogFlow is built on a structured relational data model designed specifically for the TCU Club Sports context — separating operational and restricted funds, tracking eligibility per player, and maintaining a permanent audit trail of every approval.

18
Tables

A relational data model from scratch

Designed in collaboration with ChatGPT through a structured interview process, the data model captures clubs, users, requests, approvals, ledger transactions, roster players, events, and form submissions. Each table has explicit data types and relationships built around how club sports actually operate.

2
Fund Types

Operational vs. Restricted budgets

The financial model mirrors the real distinction TCU Club Sports uses. Operational funds (from SGA allocations and dues) must be spent within the fiscal year. Restricted funds (from donations) carry over but can only be used for designated purposes. The app tracks both separately in real time.

9
Versions

Iterative prototype development

Built across nine versions of iteration, each one fixing what didn't work and adding what was missing. The major revisions added a cross-club staff dashboard, an approval confirmation flow with budget impact visibility, and a fully responsive desktop layout with sidebar navigation.

~50
Sample Records

Realistic simulated data

The prototype runs on simulated data designed to look and behave like real TCU Club Sports operations — five clubs, dozens of purchase requests at various approval stages, populated rosters with mixed eligibility statuses, and a full ledger of historical transactions. Enough to demonstrate every feature end-to-end.

What it does

Built for both sides of the relationship.

FrogFlow has two distinct experiences — one designed for student club officers running day-to-day operations, and one for Rec Center staff who need cross-club oversight.

📋

Purchase Requests

Six request types — equipment, tournament fees, hotel and travel, coach pay, referee fees, and medical supplies — each with tailored forms. Required fields enforced at submission so incomplete requests never reach staff.

💰

Live Budget Tracking

Operational and Restricted balances updated in real time. Full transaction ledger tied to specific approvals. No more emailing staff for an updated spreadsheet — the current balance is always one click away.

👥

Roster Eligibility

Per-player tracking of liability waivers and medical clearance. Players missing required documents are flagged automatically. No more manually cross-referencing rosters against form submissions.

📊

Staff Cross-Club Dashboard

For Rec Center staff: every club at a glance. Prioritized approval queue across all clubs, club health cards showing budget status, and a spending breakdown by category.

✅

Approval Confirmation Flow

Before any approval is posted, staff see the current balance, the deduction amount, and the projected balance after approval — with a low-balance warning if funds drop below $500. Auto-posts to the ledger.

🔔

Notifications Center

Instant alerts when requests are submitted, approved, denied, or sent back for more information. No more wondering whether an email got through. Status changes are visible the moment they happen.

What's next

From prototype to production.

The current prototype is a functional, interactive demonstration. The path forward is concrete: validate with the Rec Center, build a real backend, and expand from there.

1
In Progress

Get formal approval from a major university

The first real milestone is institutional buy-in — ideally from TCU. I have already started conversations with Zack Lundgren at the Campus Recreation Center, and the response has been positive. The next step is a formal demo meeting and a pilot conversation about what it would take to bring FrogFlow into the Club Sports program officially.

2
Planned

Build the production backend

Once the project has institutional support, the prototype moves from in-memory simulated data to a real, persistent backend. I have already begun looking at Lovable as the development platform and Supabase as the backend database. The architecture stays independent of TCU's internal systems — Okta SSO would be the only integration, used purely for outbound authentication.

3
Future Vision

Expand beyond Club Sports

The structural problem FrogFlow solves — fragmented administrative workflows running through email and spreadsheets — is not unique to club sports. Greek life, student government, intramurals, and other student-run organizations face the same challenges. The data model is generic enough that with modest customization, FrogFlow could expand to support other parts of student life at TCU and eventually other universities.

Grounded in research

A documented institutional gap.

The administrative challenges TCU competitive sports clubs face are not unique. Academic research on collegiate sport clubs (CSCs) consistently identifies communication gaps, leadership turnover, informal governance structures, and limited administrative support as some of the biggest barriers to effective club operations. FrogFlow is designed to address those structural problems directly.

1

Springer, D., & Dixon, M. A. (2025). Deconstructing the structure of collegiate sport clubs. Recreational Sports Journal.

Found that clubs operate with simple, flexible structures characterized by centralized decision-making, limited complexity, and informal coordination — dynamics that foster autonomy but challenge leadership sustainability across transitions.

journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/15588661251344898
2

Czekanski, W. A., & Lower-Hoppe, L. M. (2024). The role of external partners in shaping the organizational capacity of collegiate sport clubs: A Delphi study. European Sport Management Quarterly.

Identified that CSCs struggle to achieve their goals because of unique challenges in the collegiate context, including membership restrictions, university regulation, and the need for effective communication between clubs and recreational sport departments.

tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/16184742.2024.2412647
3

Lower-Hoppe, L. M., et al. (2021). The collegiate sport club model: Development of a conceptual framework. International Journal of Sport Management, 22(3).

Empirically defined the CSC model using qualitative data from 68 sport club officers, four recreational sport professional staff, and 29 university documents. Highlighted the connections between club purposes, inputs, activities, and outcomes — a framework FrogFlow's data model is structured around.

internationaljournalofsportmanagement.com/the-collegiate-sport-club-model
4

Journal of Park and Recreation Administration (2024). Collegiate sport club service delivery: Moving between motivations and constraints to beneficial outcomes.

Identified leadership turnover, communication gaps, and lack of structured knowledge as primary constraints on participant experience in collegiate club sports. Recommended that managers create opportunities to overcome these challenges through better systems and information flow.

js.sagamorepub.com/index.php/jpra/article/view/11046
